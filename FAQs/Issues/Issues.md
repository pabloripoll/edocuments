# Issues

Yes, several specific "diagnoses" describe product, design, and engineering projects going off the rails. They are categorized below by what goes wrong.

## Architectural & Code Diagnoses

* Gold Plating: Working on a feature past the point of diminishing returns. Engineers polish code or add backend optimizations that the user will never see or care about. [1]
* Architecture Creep: Over-engineering a system. Building a complex, massive infrastructure to handle millions of users before the product even has its first hundred customers.
* Software Bloat (Bloatware): The physical consequence of feature creep. The software becomes so packed with unneeded features that it uses too much memory, runs slowly, and occupies massive disk space. [2, 3]

## Project & Process Diagnoses

* Scope Creep: Unauthorized growth of a project's requirements after it has started. This is a project management failure, whereas feature creep is a product design failure. [4, 5, 6]
* Hope Creep: A team falls behind schedule but keeps reporting that everything is on time. They "hope" a miracle will happen to catch them up before the deadline. [7]
* Effort Creep: A task takes much longer than planned. The team is working hard, but the progress metrics do not match the hours logged. [8]
* Requirement Churn: Constantly changing, replacing, or redefining requirements throughout the development cycle. Work is discarded before it is even finished.

## Psychological & Organizational Diagnoses

* Shiny Object Syndrome: Leadership or product managers getting distracted by the newest tech trends (e.g., forcing AI, Web3, or VR into a simple app) instead of fixing core user problems.
* Design by Committee: A product that tries to please every stakeholder. Because no one has the power to veto, the final design becomes a messy compromise that satisfies no one.
* Analysis Paralysis: Overthinking the product design or features to the point that the team becomes paralyzed and never actually builds or ships anything.
